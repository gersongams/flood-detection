# Sentinel-1 Spatiotemporal Simulation Using Convolutional LSTM for Flood Mapping

> **TL;DR** — Instead of comparing a single pre-event SAR image to a post-event image, the authors train a ConvLSTM on a *historical time series* of Sentinel-1 intensity images to **forecast the next scene** (a "synthetic" pre-event image). The Delta image (simulated − observed post-event) is then thresholded to produce a Flood Proxy Map. Thresholding the ConvLSTM Delta gave the highest Cohen's Kappa in all three case studies: **0.92 for Australia, 0.78 for Mozambique, and 0.68 for Brazil** per the abstract (p. 1) — though Table 5 (p. 18) reports 0.93/0.92 (Australia VV/VH), 0.75/0.78 (Mozambique VV/VH), 0.51/0.66 (Brumadinho VV/VH). ConvLSTM beat the vanilla LSTM, the last-pre-event-image baseline, and the historical-mean baseline in every case.

## Problem & Motivation
Standard SAR flood mapping compares one pre-event and one post-event scene and thresholds the difference (a "Flood Proxy Map", FPM). This yields commission/omission errors because **not all land-cover change is flood-induced**: crop growth/harvest, forest clearance, and urban changes all alter backscatter (p. 2). Pair-based change detection "fail[s] to take the seasonal variability into account" (p. 2).

Their hypothesis: analyzing the *historical time series* per pixel lets a model learn the expected backscatter for the next timestep; a flood then appears as an outlier the model could not have forecast. To keep the **spatial** structure (which a per-pixel LSTM destroys when it unravels the raster into a 1-D vector), they use ConvLSTM (Shi et al.). The stated goal (p. 2) is a Flood Proxy Mapping framework that can leverage Google Earth Engine for on-demand scalability.

## Method / Architecture

Four models compared, each producing a Delta (Δσ°) against the post-event scene t_{n+1} (Eqs. 1–4, p. 4):
- **B1 (baseline)**: Δ = last pre-event image − post-event.
- **B2 (baseline)**: Δ = per-pixel historical mean of the whole stack − post-event.
- **L1 (RNN LSTM)**: Δ = LSTM-forecast image − post-event.
- **L2 (ConvLSTM)**: Δ = ConvLSTM-forecast image − post-event.

### ConvLSTM (L2) — the architecture of interest (p. 3)
- **2 stacked ConvLSTM layers, 20 cells each**, Glorot uniform init, **tanh** activation.
- Each ConvLSTM layer: **conv filter size 3, stride 1**.
- **Top layer: a single 3D convolutional layer, filter size 3, stride 1, no activation**, generating the final prediction (the next frame).
- Stated **receptive field of 7**: "each value in the model's output has been generated by taking into consideration a region of 7 × 7 pixels from the model's input" (p. 3).
- **Loss: MSE. Optimizer: Adam. LR = 1e-3 (fixed). Batch size = 8. Max 20 epochs with early stopping. L2 regularization, rate 1e-2** (p. 3).
- **Input tensors**: image stacks cropped into patches of **50 × 50 px (Brumadinho)** and **100 × 100 px (Australia, Mozambique)**; "each patch preserved the data across the time domain" (pp. 3–4) — i.e. the full depth of the stack is the sequence dimension.
- **Sequence length / number of timesteps**: *not reported explicitly as a hyperparameter.* It is the depth of each site's stack (13 scenes Australia, 53 Mozambique, 29 Brumadinho; Table 1, p. 7), minus the post-event scene. The validation experiment (§5.1.1, p. 9) used **21 S1 scenes** (4 April → 8 Dec 2017) to train and predicted the 22 Dec 2017 scene. The paper's own closing sentence admits this is unresolved: "further studies could be conducted to evaluate the correlation between the depth of stack and model performance" (p. 22).
- **Prediction scheme**: sequence-to-one — given the pre-event sequence t_0…t_n, output a single synthetic frame for t_{n+1}. One model per polarization (VV and VH trained separately).
- **Split: 70-20-10 train/validation/test** (p. 4).
- The Australian training set (13 images over ~3588 km²) was patched into 1 km² tiles yielding **2336 training samples** (p. 4). Two reasons given for patching: enough samples to detect over/under-fitting, and GPU RAM limits.

### LSTM (L1) baseline (p. 3)
- 3-D stack (rows × cols × depth) unravelled to a 2-D array (rows×cols) × depth — **each pixel is an independent univariate time-series sample**; sequence-to-vector, predicts backscatter at t_{n+1}.
- **3 stacked LSTM layers, 20 cells**, Glorot uniform, tanh; then a **single dense neuron, no activation**.
- Max **20 epochs**, Adam, **LR 1e-3**, early stopping after **5** non-improving iterations, **MSE loss**, **L2 reg 1e-2** per layer.
- Output 1-D vector reshaped back to a 2-D raster.
- **Batch size for L1: not reported.**

### Post-processing (pp. 8–9)
- **Grid search over paired positive/negative thresholds** on the Delta image (increments of ±0.25 dB in the Australia case, p. 11). Pixels above the positive threshold OR below the negative threshold are labeled flooded. Both signs are used because open water lowers backscatter (specular reflection) while double-bounce in flooded urban/vegetated areas raises it.
- Metrics: Recall, Precision, F1, Critical Score Index (CSI), Cohen's Kappa (Eqs. 5–10, p. 8). Kappa chosen as the main metric because it was "more sensitive to threshold changes" (p. 12).
- **Multi-threshold transfer**: the 10 threshold pairs with highest Kappa in Australia were applied to Mozambique, stacked, and the per-pixel detection frequency became a relative-frequency (likelihood) flood map (pp. 9, 19).
- Implementation: **TensorFlow 2.0, Python 3, Google Colab Pro GPU** (p. 5).

## Data

- **Sensor: Sentinel-1 GRD SAR** (not optical). **VV and VH polarizations, modeled separately.**
- Australia & Mozambique: **Sigma-naught (σ°)** GRD from **Google Earth Engine**. Brumadinho: **Gamma-naught Radiometric Terrain Corrected (γ°_t)** from ASF Vertex (terrain correction needed for the hilly site) (p. 7).
- **Spatial resolution: not explicitly reported** (S1 GRD in GEE is 10 m; the paper does not state it). Reference Planet Labs imagery is 4 m (p. 7).

| Site | Area (km²) | # Scenes | Stack start | Stack end | Path/Frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Australia (TC Debbie) | 3588.71 | 13 | 26 Oct 2016 | 19 Mar 2017 | 79/657 |
| Mozambique (TC Idai) | 276.48 | 53 | 21 Feb 2017 | 07 Mar 2019 | 155/660 |
| Brumadinho (dam collapse) | 21.90 | 29 | 23 Nov 2017 | 17 Jan 2019 | 118/669 |

(Table 1, p. 7). Post-event target images: 31 March 2017 for Australia (Table 2, p. 11).

- **Ground truth / reference**: Australia + Mozambique — flood masks from **pre/post Sentinel-2 water masks contrasted with Otsu thresholding**; Brumadinho — **manually digitized from 4 m Planet Labs imagery** (p. 7). Optical workflow (Fig. 2, p. 5) is: S2 optical → cloud masking → Otsu thresholding → reference FPM. The authors explicitly caveat that accuracy is *relative to optical-derived reference*, not in-situ data (p. 7).
- **Spectral indices**: **none used in the main method** (SAR intensity only). The prior work they compare against (Ulloa et al. 2020, ref [40]) used **NDSI (Normalized Difference Sigma-Naught Index)** and **SNDSI (Shannon's Entropy of NDSI)** — formulas *not restated in this paper*.
- **Preprocessing**: no speckle filtering is mentioned (**not reported**); no normalization scheme is described (**not reported**); values are in dB. Terrain correction only for Brumadinho (RTC product).

|---|---|---|---|
| VV | 0.310615 | 1.432977 | 1.466256 |
| VH | 0.799748 | 1.714616 | 1.89196 |

ConvLSTM wins in all six rows. Improvement over baselines at Brumadinho: "almost 10% when compared to the baseline models, and 5% to LSTM" (p. 19). Mozambique baselines B1/B2 were **not run** (dashes in Table 5).

**Selected thresholds**: Australia VV, Δσ° ≥ 2 and ≤ −11 (p. 13); Mozambique, positive 3 dB (VV) / 5 dB (VH), negative −8 dB both (p. 17); Brumadinho VH, Δγ° ≥ 1.2 and ≤ −1.6 dB (p. 18).

**Threshold transfer (Australia → Mozambique, multi-threshold relative frequency map)** (p. 20): using cut values of 0.5 and 0.9 on the relative-frequency map, **VV gave Kappa 0.75 and 0.73; VH gave 0.58 and 0.69**. VH transferred poorly (larger commission error), attributed to volumetric scattering differences in the land cover between sites (pp. 20–21).

**Error analysis** (p. 14, Table 4 p. 15): ConvLSTM **overestimates** flood extent — false-positive area was **72.6% larger than false-negative area for VV, and 48% larger for VH**. Dominant error class is **herbaceous vegetation (61.92% of FP VV)**, then open/closed forest — "using C-band for flood mapping over vegetated areas remains a challenging task" (p. 14).

⚠️ **Inconsistencies flagged:**
1. **Abstract vs Table 5 / §5.1.4.** Abstract (p. 1) says "0.92 for Australia, 0.78 for Mozambique, and 0.68 for Brazil". Table 5 (p. 18) and the Conclusions (p. 21) give Australia 0.93 (VV) / 0.92 (VH), Mozambique 0.75 (VV) / 0.78 (VH), and Brumadinho **0.66** (VH) — not 0.68. The 0.68 Brazil figure in the abstract is unsupported anywhere else in the paper.
2. **Mozambique text vs Table 5.** §5.1.3 (p. 17) states "the highest Kappa values can achieve **0.72 and 0.75**" for VV and VH from the ConvLSTM figures, while Table 5 assigns 0.75/0.78 to ConvLSTM and 0.72/0.74 to LSTM. The p. 17 text appears to quote the wrong row.

## Limitations
Authors' own:
- Reference "ground truth" is derived from optical imagery, not in situ; accuracy is therefore relative (p. 7).
- Brumadinho underperforms (Kappa 0.51–0.66) — attributed to small area (21.9 km² → too few training samples), rugged topography (15–30° slopes → layover/shadow artifacts), and dense forest whose backscatter varies naturally (p. 19).
- Thresholds do not transfer across sites when land cover differs; they "would need to be reassessed" (p. 21).
- Stack depth vs performance was never studied — explicitly listed as future work (p. 22).

Observed by me:
- **No held-out independent test event** in the strict sense: thresholds are chosen by grid search *against the reference map itself*, so the reported Kappas are best-case/oracle thresholds, not blind predictions. The only genuinely transferred setting (Australia→Mozambique) degrades (VH to 0.58).
- Only 3 events, one post-event image each; n = 3 for generalization claims.
- No uncertainty quantification, no repeated runs / seeds, no confidence intervals.
- Sequence length is never treated as a hyperparameter; the model is retrained per site, so nothing is learned across sites.
- No speckle filtering or normalization is documented — a reproducibility gap.
- ConvLSTM's edge over the trivial historical-mean baseline B2 in Australia is modest (0.93 vs 0.91 VV).

## Relevance to this thesis

**High — this is the closest architectural match to the thesis's temporal framing.** Directly borrowable:

- **Architecture, verbatim-adoptable in PyTorch.** 2 stacked ConvLSTM layers × 20 hidden cells, 3×3 kernels, stride 1, tanh, topped by a **Conv3D(kernel 3, stride 1, no activation)** head that emits the next frame. Loss **MSE**, Adam, LR 1e-3, batch 8, ≤20 epochs, early stopping, weight decay 1e-2. This is a concrete, small, cheap recipe you can reproduce on a laptop/Colab — a good first "temporal" model for the thesis before anything fancier.
- **The core idea is portable from SAR intensity to NDVI/NDWI.** Feed a sequence of NDVI/NDWI rasters (t_0…t_n), forecast t_{n+1}, and compute a **Delta image = predicted − observed**. Under the thesis's disaster-management framing, an anomalously **high observed NDWI vs predicted NDWI** (or a collapse in NDVI) is exactly the "flood-induced outlier" signal. Multi-channel input (stack NDVI+NDWI as 2 channels, like their VV/VH but jointly) is a natural thesis extension over their per-polarization models.
- **Patching + split recipe.** Crop each scene stack into **50×50 or 100×100 px patches preserving the full time depth**, split **70/20/10**. Their Australia case turned 13 scenes over 3588 km² into 2336 patch samples — a direct answer to "how do I get enough training samples from 5 years of Sentinel-2 over one AOI in Peru?"
- **The two baselines are cheap and should be replicated in the thesis.** B1 = last pre-event image; **B2 = per-pixel historical mean of the stack**. B2 is a *strong* baseline (Kappa 0.91 vs ConvLSTM 0.93). If the thesis's CNN cannot beat "mean NDWI of the past 5 years minus the current NDWI", the deep learning adds nothing. This is the single most actionable takeaway.
- **Metric choice.** Use **Cohen's Kappa** (and CSI), not accuracy/F1 alone: with a huge non-flooded majority class, their F1/precision/recall saturate above 0.97 for *every* model including the naive one (Table 3, p. 12). This is a cautionary example for how the thesis reports results.
- **The paired positive/negative threshold grid search** on the Delta image, plus the **Kappa heatmap** over (positive, negative) threshold pairs, is a reusable and easy-to-implement evaluation artifact. Caveat: they tune thresholds against the reference map, so the thesis should hold out an event if it copies this.
- **Reference-mask generation** matches the thesis stack directly: Sentinel-2 → cloud mask → water index → **Otsu thresholding** → binary flood reference. This is a ready-made way to build labels for the Peru AOI without in-situ data.

**Where it differs / limits transfer:**
- **SAR (Sentinel-1) not optical.** The thesis's biggest advantage over this paper is that clouds do not block SAR — this paper never has to deal with missing timesteps. **A 10%-cloud-filtered Sentinel-2 series is irregularly sampled and gappy**; ConvLSTM assumes a regular sequence. The thesis must decide on temporal interpolation/gap-filling or masked loss — a problem this paper does not solve for you.
- Task is **inundation mapping of a single event**, not multi-year **susceptibility / prediction of flood-prone areas**. If the thesis's goal is susceptibility, this paper is a *method to cite* for the temporal encoder, not an end-to-end template.
- They forecast **one step ahead** only. The thesis's "predict flood-prone areas" may want a longer horizon or a risk score rather than a next-frame reconstruction.
- Their strongest cautionary finding — **error concentrates in vegetated land cover (61.9% of false positives on herbaceous vegetation)** — is C-band-specific. With optical NDVI/NDWI the failure modes differ (cloud shadow, turbid water), but the lesson that *land cover conditions the error, and thresholds do not transfer between land-cover regimes*, applies directly to a Peruvian AOI spanning coast/sierra/selva.

**Classification: primary method to cite + a baseline design (B2 historical mean) + a metric convention (Kappa).** Not a dataset source.

## Keywords / Tech
- **Models**: ConvLSTM (Shi et al. 2015), stacked LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber), Conv3D output head; baselines = last-pre-event image, historical per-pixel mean.
- **Sensors**: Sentinel-1 GRD (σ°, VV/VH), Sentinel-1 RTC (γ°_t), Sentinel-2 (reference), Planet Labs 4 m (reference).
- **Indices**: none in this paper's method; NDSI/SNDSI in the authors' cited prior work [40].
- **Frameworks**: TensorFlow 2.0, Python 3, Google Colab Pro (GPU), Google Earth Engine, ASF Vertex.
- **Techniques**: next-frame spatiotemporal forecasting, change detection via Delta image, Flood Proxy Map, Otsu thresholding, paired positive/negative threshold grid search, multi-threshold relative-frequency mapping, Glorot init, L2 regularization, early stopping.
- **Metrics**: Cohen's Kappa, CSI (threat score), F1, precision, recall, RMSE.
